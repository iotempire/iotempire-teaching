This introduction is adapted for the condensed **HSBI/GT block course**. It is primarily about helping you understand **how the class works**, what is expected from you, how assessment is structured, and how the workshop modules fit together.

The course has a strong emphasis on **MCU programming and embedded systems work**. **IoT** is still an important part of the class, but it is treated as one important application area and system context rather than the sole focus of the course.

## How to use this workbook

This repository contains tasks, references, and suggested learning paths for the course. You do not have to complete every listed task mechanically. Instead, you should aim to meet the requirements of each module, document your work clearly, and use the material here as guidance and support.

Your goal is to accomplish the module outcomes and document your learning - not to tick off every single bullet point without reflection.

## Assessment structure

This course uses a **combination exam** based on three connected parts:

- **Portfolio**
  - your ongoing documentation of practical work, code, notes, pictures, results, and selected evidence from the modules
- **Reflections**
  - short reflective writing after class blocks about your learning, struggles, progress, collaboration, and takeaways
- **Final project**
  - a final system or prototype that builds on the methods, tools, and ideas developed during the course

These three parts belong together. The portfolio documents what you built, the reflections document how you learned, and the final project demonstrates how you can apply and combine what you learned.

## Tasks

- Take brief notes during class, and optionally pictures of hardware, for personal recollection and later portfolio use.
- Set up your personal git-based portfolio repository:
  - create a new repository on [github.com](http://github.com) via “Use this template” or by forking [https://portfolio.iotempower.us](https://portfolio.iotempower.us)
  - read the instructions in the template carefully, especially the guidance on writing **reflections**
  - share the repo with the instructor(s) and permit at least read access for **Ulno**
  - add your first notes from class into the portfolio
  - if work is documented jointly, document it once and let others link to it instead of duplicating content
- Prepare for reflections:
  - understand that reflections are a regular required part of the course
  - plan to write them soon after each workshop day while your impressions are still fresh
  - connect reflections to both technical work and collaboration experience

## Reflection prompts

Your reflections should ideally be written on the day of class or very soon afterwards.

Address at least some of the following:

- What do you want to remember from this class block?
- What was clear and useful?
- What was difficult or confusing?
- Where did you struggle, explore, or get stuck?
- How do you evaluate your own work and participation?
- How did collaboration with peers and instructors go?
- What help did you receive or give?
- What should you do differently in the next block?

The original workbook also explicitly suggested addressing:

- what was good
- what was difficult
- whether there was any good/fun struggling or exploration
- whether there were any show stoppers where you should reach out to peers or instructors
- self-evaluation
- interaction with peers and instructors
- help and extra work, including who helped you and whom you helped
- whether you presented, implemented, made a PR, or fixed something important for the class itself

## Homework note for the block format

Reflections are always homework. If you do not finish things in class, continue them soon after class, ideally together with your partner, so that your documentation and technical progress stay current.
