## What to expect

The room is a fair, not a stage. In each 90-minute block, **five teams** stand at tables with a
laptop, a poster or a few slides, and a 2-minute recorded fallback. They present repeatedly while
the rest of the room visits, takes notes for the final reflection, praises one thing, and gives one
concrete suggestion. The second five teams set up during the break and present in the second block.

The instructor and TA circulate. Each table gets a **15-minute assessed slot — 10 minutes live demo
and 5 minutes of questions** — with a hard clock. The TA gives feedback and may suggest points or
mark an individual for recheck; the instructor records the points.

**Announce the format before the day.** Both 90-minute blocks are demo/feedback time; there is no
new content. Students who are not presenting are working too: they visit tables and write their
final reflection.

Three predictable events:

1. **A live demo crashes.** The module requires a fallback recording. A crash handled calmly and
   explained is fine; a crash nobody can explain costs the team its demonstration points.
2. **One member cannot answer.** “That was my teammate’s part” is explicitly not an answer. Record
   it as an individual note — it does not sink the team, but it matters.
3. **Demos built on slides.** Stop them politely and ask for the query. The rubric says “slides may
   support, never replace, the live run”.

## Before the session

- [ ] Set up the **first five tables** before class; mark each with the team name and a table number.
- [ ] Put the **second five tables** in the break plan; teams set them up while the room is on break.
- [ ] Post the table map and the block order. Tell each team which block it presents in.
- [ ] Clone every repository onto the demo machine beforehand; do not debug Git during a discussion.
- [ ] Print or open the rubric with a column per team; write individual notes as you go.
- [ ] Check the projector with a **terminal** — the demo is text, and small fonts are the usual
      failure.
- [ ] Brief the TA: **two training rounds** — the TA joins you at the tables in both blocks, asks
      the questions, writes feedback, and may suggest points. The TA does not record the grade.
- [ ] Ask teams to bring power strips and a 2-minute fallback video.

Each table slot is **10 minutes live demo + 5 minutes questions**. The clock is visible and hard:
when the 15 minutes end, you move to the next table, even mid-sentence. The next team starts while
you sit down.

The **TA joins you for both blocks** — those are the two training rounds. After this run, the TA can
take a table lane independently while you take the other; the instructor still records every point.

## Asking project-discussion questions

One question per member, aimed at the piece they own. With 5–6 members, the 5-minute question block
gives each person about a minute. Have a short list ready per role:

- **Schema owner:** "Which constraint would you add first if you had one more hour, and what bad row
  does it stop?"
- **Ingestion owner:** "Run the loader twice. What guarantees no duplicates?"
- **Query owner:** "Show the plan. What did the index change, and what did it not change?"
- **Interface owner:** "What happens when a row is missing or malformed?"
- **Docs owner:** "Rebuild it from the README, out loud. Which step is missing?"
- **Anyone:** "What would you change if you started over, and why?"

**Be amazed on purpose.** Name one thing that impressed you at every table; the fair is also a
celebration of what the teams built. Then ask the question that matters.

## Visitors — the final reflection

Students who are not presenting are not idle:

- Visit at least three tables per block.
- Write one thing that impressed you, one question you asked, and one concrete suggestion.
- Use the notes in the final reflection; the visit is part of the learning, not a break.

## Scoring

Use the [rubric](../modules/08-final-project.md) as written — **3.0 code and system documentation,
2.0 demo / discussion**. Do not re-derive the criteria at the fair; consistency across teams matters
more than precision on any one.

Write the individual-discussion note next to the team score. The TA may suggest points and mark an
individual for recheck; the instructor confirms and records. That note, plus the checkpoints and the
portfolio, is the basis for the final conversation.

## Retrospective (10 minutes, after the last table)

Three questions, written, one per person, anonymous if they prefer:

1. What did this course make you understand that a lecture would not have?
2. Where did the studio format waste your time?
3. What should the next cohort do differently in week one?

Read them before you plan the next run. The syllabus promises the class moves; this is where you
collect the evidence for how.

## Where it goes wrong

- **No hard clock.** Five tables × 15 minutes is 75 minutes; the 15-minute buffer disappears if you
  let one table run. Move on the timer.
- **Visitors do not reflect.** Without the note requirement, the non-presenting teams drift. Ask for
  one praise, one question, one suggestion per table.
- **One table hogs the instructor.** The TA is there to keep the other tables moving; use the
  training rounds to calibrate, then split the tables.
- **Grading the polish.** Posters and production value are not in the rubric; a working query and a
  clear explanation are.
- **Skipping the individual part** because the team demo went well. The individual project
  discussion is where the learning goals are actually verified.
- **The TA records points.** The TA may suggest and mark rechecks; the instructor records.
- **No record.** If you do not write the note during the fair, you will reconstruct it from
  impression three weeks later, which is exactly the sieve this course is trying to avoid.
