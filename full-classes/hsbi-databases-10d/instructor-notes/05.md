## What to expect

This is the session where the old lecture said "normalize to 3NF, it is the right thing". Here students **measure** a trade-off instead — and the measurement is less dramatic than the story suggests. On 200,000 rows the dashboard runs in **tens of milliseconds either way**; the visible costs are **storage** (about 2.7×) and the **4,000-row update** of a duplicated fact. Expect at least one pod to conclude "the normalized one is fine" — that is a legitimate, defensible result, not a failed lab.

The concept that produces the most argument is the **functional dependency**, not the normal forms. A pod will claim `machine_id → site` is "obvious" — ask them whether the five sample rows prove it or the domain does. That distinction is the actual skill.

Also expect: `dbstat` is not available in every SQLite build. The storage query is optional, not a requirement; if it errors, say so and move on.

## Opening (5 minutes)

Yasmin Kaya, the operations lead, asks for the shortcut: copy `site` and `technician` onto every row and add a total. She is right about the read and wrong about the writes. Then: *"Today you do both, and you measure the difference so the decision is not a matter of taste."* That framing makes the whole session about evidence rather than dogma.

## Running the studio

- **Task A (study, 30 min, hard stop).** The five-row extract is the warm-up; the real work is on the 200,000-row table. Have pods mark each FD as *domain fact* or *observed accident* — that column is where the thinking shows. At minute 30 they stop and start the lab, finished or not.
- **Task B (share, 15 min).** Compare FD lists across pods. Every pod will have one FD the others do not, and arguing it out is the point.
- **Task 1 (dependencies and anomalies).** Ask for the *count of rows* an update touches. A number is convincing; "many rows" is not.
- **Task 2 (3NF).** The integrity check — `flat_rows == normalized_rows` — is the deliverable. If they differ, the decomposition lost data; that is the lesson.
- **Task 3 (benchmark).** The read is usually close; **storage and the update tell the stronger story**. The update changes **1 row versus 4,000** — make them say that number out loud. Also have them drop `ix_report_machine` and re-run: on this dataset the query gets *faster* without it, which is a second negative result.
- **Task 4 (decide and defend).** Force a one-sentence decision *with the number that justifies it*. "It depends" is not an answer; "we chose X because the dashboard runs 4×/hour and the write path 40×/minute" is.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Foreign keys are off**, so an insert into `job_part` with a missing part *succeeds* and creates a dangling reference. The lab no longer does this, but a student experimenting will. Link it back to Module 4.
- **Comparing the wrong thing.** The normalized dashboard joins four tables; the denormalized one scans one. That is not apples to apples — and noticing that is a higher-level insight than the timing itself.
- **The index on the report table hurts.** `SCAN report_jobpart USING INDEX ix_report_machine` walks the index and then fetches 200,000 rows from the table. Dropping the index makes it faster. If nobody finds this, ask them to.
- **Milliseconds, not 40 seconds.** The story is about a production dataset; the lab is 200,000 rows. Students will measure ~20–30 ms and think they did it wrong. Say it before they do.
- **Copying prices into the report table** and then changing a price: the report now lies. Demonstrate it once; it is the whole point of the session.
- **`SUM` over a text price.** SQLite will coerce it and the number will look right — until the sort order surprises them.

## Harvest

**Collections, not explanations.** Nudge them toward: `printf('%.2f', …)` storing money as text, the `CROSS JOIN` that turns 20,000 × 10 into 200,000 rows, and any functional dependency they disagree about. Swap at the break and at the end.
