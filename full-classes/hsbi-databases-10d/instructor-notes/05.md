## What to expect

**There is no pod study any more.** The first 15 minutes are a **skim**, not a read: students look at the seven rounds and the theory boxes, then start Round 1. Expect the room to be spread across Rounds 2–4 by the break — that is the design, not a failure.

**Rough block plan (180 minutes, 2 × 90).** skim 15 → See it 25 → Break it 20 → Build it 50 → Race it 70. The break falls inside Round 3. **Round 4 is the class boundary**: if the room reaches the end of Round 4, the session succeeded. Rounds 5–7 are homework; pods that finish early start Round 5 in class.

These estimates are still optimistic — the first run overran them. **Double them again for the next cohort** and keep Round 4 as the cut line.

The gates are the only synchronisation. Two minutes, neighbour to neighbour, no instructor. Your job is to walk, listen at the gates, and answer only when a neighbour cannot. If you find yourself explaining normal forms to a whole table, you have become the lecture this format replaced.

## The gate board

Pods down one side, rounds across the top. A tick when a pod has passed a gate. It costs nothing and it shows you the room at a glance: a pod stuck on Round 1 for 25 minutes needs a nudge; a pod on Round 5 is fine.

## The moment the room stops believing you: B is slower than A

On this dataset the denormalized query is often **slower** than the four-way join. Students read that as “I did it wrong”. Say before they measure: *“I expect the one-table version to lose here. If it wins for you, that is also a result — write down why.”*

**Why it happens (three reasons, in order of impact):**

1. **`ix_report_machine`.** The plan becomes `SCAN report_jobpart USING INDEX` — walk the whole index, then fetch 200,000 rows from the table. That is more work than one sequential scan. Dropping the index usually makes B *faster*.
2. **Row width.** `report_jobpart` carries site, technician, price and date on every row, roughly 3× the bytes of `job_part`. Same number of rows, more pages.
3. **Warm cache and ms scale.** 50 machines, 10 parts, 20,000 jobs all fit in cache, so the join is cheap. At 20–40 ms, the order of measurement decides the answer.

**Make them measure properly:** A and B **interleaved**, three runs each, compare medians. A naive “run A, run B once” produces a coin flip, and the room will argue about it for ten minutes.

**The one sentence you want out of the room:** *denormalization pays when the **join** is the expensive part — not when the table is merely smaller.*

**Have the counter-demo ready** (the optional try in Round 4). Add two dimensions (`vendor`, `site_dim`), rebuild the wider report table, and run the six-join A against the wide-table B:

- A ≈ **45 ms**, B ≈ **29 ms** — B wins clearly, and the plan for B is a plain `SCAN` plus a temp B-tree.
- Both queries return **3,500 rows, total 722,610.0** — verifying equality with `EXCEPT` is a good two-minute demo in itself.
- State the caveats out loud: the rebuild dropped the index, and 200,000 rows still fit in memory. It is a direction, not a production number.

**After the optional try:** `CREATE INDEX ix_report_machine ON report_jobpart(machine_id);` — otherwise Round 5's deliberate `DROP INDEX` has nothing to drop.

Do not let this become a lecture on cost models. The takeaway is that “normalized vs denormalized” is not a rule but a **measurement with a workload attached**, which is exactly what their memo has to argue.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Foreign keys are off**, so an insert into `job_part` with a missing part *succeeds* and creates a dangling reference. Round 3 now asks them to turn the pragma on once. Link it back to Module 4.
- **The index on the report table hurts.** `SCAN report_jobpart USING INDEX ix_report_machine` walks the index and then fetches every row from the table. Dropping it is usually faster. If nobody finds this, ask them to — it is the first of the three reasons in the section above.
- **“B is slower than A” panic.** Covered above; the short answer is index + row width + warm cache, and the fix is interleaved measurement.
- **Milliseconds, not 40 seconds.** The story is about a production dataset; the lab is 200,000 rows. Students will measure ~20–30 ms and think they did it wrong. Say it before they do.
- **Comparing the wrong thing.** The normalized dashboard joins four tables; the denormalized one scans one. That is not apples to apples — and noticing that is a higher-level insight than the timing itself.
- **Copying prices into the report table** and then changing a price: the report now lies. Demonstrate it once; it is the whole point of the session.
- **`SUM` over a text price.** SQLite will coerce it and the number will look right — until the sort order surprises them.
- **Skipping the skim.** A pod that starts at Round 3 without the primer will build the wrong tables and come back confused. Two minutes of “what will I produce?” saves twenty.
